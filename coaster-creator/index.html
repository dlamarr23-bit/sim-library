How to use this simulation
  1. Cart Design. Give your coaster a name. Pick a pattern, a main color and a detail color. Press Next.
  2. Cart Length. Use the slider to choose how many cars are in the train. Watch the mass change in the PE and KE equations at the top.
  3. Track Design. The first hill is the chain lift. Click to the right of the last node to add more track. Drag any black node to make hills and valleys. Select a node and press Add loop to build a loop there. Keep going until your track reaches COASTER END.
  4. Coaster Test. Press Play. The chain pulls the train up the first hill, then lets it go. Watch the bar: red PE is potential energy, green KE is kinetic energy, and orange TE is thermal energy made by friction and air.
  5. Read the numbers. The equations at the top fill in with the train's mass, height and speed as it moves. Press Whole track to see the full ride at once.
  6. Fix and try again. If the train stalls or crashes, press Back, change the track, and test it again.
  7. Keyboard: in Track Design, click the picture, then use Tab to pick a node, arrow keys to move it (hold Shift for bigger steps), L for a loop and Delete to remove it. In Coaster Test, Space plays and pauses.
The science behind it

Potential energy (PE) is energy stored by height. A heavier train, or a higher train, stores more: PE = m · g · h. Here g is 9.81 m/s², the pull of gravity.

Kinetic energy (KE) is energy of motion: KE = ½ · m · v². Speed is squared, so going twice as fast means four times the kinetic energy.

The chain lift does work on the train. It pulls it up the first hill and gives it all of its energy. After that, nothing adds energy. As the train drops, PE turns into KE. As it climbs, KE turns back into PE.

Thermal energy (TE) is heat. Friction in the wheels and air rubbing on the train slowly turn some energy into heat. That energy is not destroyed, but it can't move the train anymore. So no hill after the lift can be as tall as the lift hill.

A loop needs speed. At the top, the train is upside down. It must be moving fast enough to stay pressed against the track. Big loops need more speed than small ones.

Force on riders is measured in g. At 1 g you feel your normal weight. At the bottom of a drop or loop you feel heavier. Over a hilltop you can feel weightless (airtime). Real coasters stay under about 5 g. This sim calls anything over 6 g a crash.

The model (details)

The track is a smooth curve through your nodes. Loops are teardrop-shaped, like real ones. Their curve starts gently and gets tightest at the top, so riders aren't slammed at the bottom. The chain lift moves the train at a steady 3 m/s, so the energy at the top of the lift is mgh + ½mv². After the lift, rolling friction (μ × the force between the wheels and track) and air drag turn energy into heat. Friction grows with mass but air drag does not, so a heavier train coasts a little farther. The train is tracked by its front car.
