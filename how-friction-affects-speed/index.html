How to use this simulation

Step-by-step directions. Click to hide or show.
  1. Pick a surface. Click one of the four buttons under "Surface on the counter." That surface covers the counter at the bottom of the ramp.
  2. Set the ramp height. Drag the Ramp height slider. The clamp slides up or down the ring stand. Pick one height and keep it the same for every surface. That keeps the test fair.
  3. Press Release car. The blue car always starts at the top of the ramp. It rolls down three times in a row: Trial 1, Trial 2 and Trial 3.
  4. Watch the timer. The timer starts when the car reaches the bottom of the ramp. It records a time each time the car passes a mark (15, 30, 45, 60 and 75 cm).
  5. Read the data table. Each surface gets three trials and an average. The three times are close but not exactly the same, just like in a real lab. "Stopped" means the car stopped before it reached that mark.
  6. Test every surface. Do them one at a time, or press Test all 4 surfaces. The graph draws one line for each surface using the average times.
  7. Change the speed or start over. The Playback slider makes the car move slower or faster on screen. Stop ends a run. Clear data erases the table and the graph (click it twice).

The science: friction and speed

Key words and why the car slows down. Click to open.

When the car leaves the ramp, it is moving fast. Nothing pushes it forward after that. On the flat surface, friction is the force that slows it down until it stops. Different surfaces make different amounts of friction.

Friction
A force between two surfaces that touch and rub. It always pushes against the motion.
Inertia
An object keeps doing what it is doing unless a force changes it. A rolling car would keep rolling if nothing slowed it down.
Contact points
The places where the wheels touch the surface. Rough or soft surfaces grab the wheels in more places. Smooth surfaces grab in fewer places.
Mass
How much matter is in an object, measured in grams (g). The car is always 60 g, so mass stays the same in this test.
Coefficient of friction (μ)
A number that tells how much two surfaces grab each other. A bigger number means more friction. The Greek letter μ is said "mew."
Trial and average
A trial is one run of the test. To find the average, add the three times, then divide by 3.

Why do three trials? Real measurements change a little every time. The car may be let go a tiny bit differently. The surface may have a small bump. One odd result could fool you. Doing three trials and using the average gives you a result you can trust more.

Why keep the ramp height the same? A taller ramp gives the car more speed at the bottom. If you change the height and the surface at the same time, you can't tell which one changed the result.

The model behind the simulation

The math the simulation uses. Click to open.

Speed at the bottom of the ramp. A taller ramp means a faster car. The 0.88 means the ramp's own friction takes away a little of the car's energy on the way down.

v = √(2 × g × h × 0.88)

Slowing down on the surface. Friction slows the car at a steady rate, a. A grabbier surface (bigger μ) slows it faster.

a = μ × g

Time to reach a mark. The time t to roll a distance d after leaving the ramp:

t = (v − √(v² − 2ad)) ÷ a

Trial-to-trial wobble. Each trial changes the launch speed by about 1% and the friction by up to about 3%. That is why the three trials are close but not the same. Here g = 980 cm/s² and h is the ramp height in cm.
